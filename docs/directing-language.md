# Directing language for clipme

This document provides a compact professional directing vocabulary so AI agents make decisions intentionally rather than generating random attractive shots.

## 1. Directing starts with change

A scene should contain a meaningful change:
- knowledge
- power
- emotion
- decision
- relationship
- physical state
- audience expectation

If nothing changes, justify the scene as atmosphere, orientation, suspense, ritual, or deliberate pause.

## 2. Scene objective

Before selecting a camera:

1. What does the scene need to accomplish?
2. Whose experience organizes the scene?
3. What should the audience know before it?
4. What should be different after it?
5. What information should be withheld?
6. What should be seen versus heard?
7. What is the exit impulse into the next scene?

## 3. Blocking

Blocking is the arrangement and movement of people/objects in space.

Use blocking to express:
- dominance/submission
- closeness/distance
- alliance/separation
- attention/distraction
- change of control
- reveal/conceal

Do not move subjects only to make the frame busy.

## 4. Shot size as meaning

- **EWS/ELS**: world, isolation, scale, geography
- **WS/LS**: body action and spatial relationships
- **MS**: interaction, gesture, balanced context
- **MCU**: conversational intimacy and reaction
- **CU**: thought/emotion/critical detail
- **ECU**: extreme emphasis, abstraction, sensory detail
- **Insert**: object/data/detail required by story
- **POV**: subjective seeing; use intentionally

A change in shot size should usually correspond to a change in dramatic/informational pressure.

## 5. Camera angle

- eye-level: observational neutrality or parity
- low: scale, power, threat, awe
- high: vulnerability, pattern, geography
- top-down: system/pattern/object relation
- profile: separation, geometry, observation
- POV: subjective alignment
- dutch/oblique: instability only when motivated

Avoid using angles as clichés without scene context.

## 6. Lens intent

Do not require a physical lens when rendering synthetically, but preserve lens semantics.

- wide: spatial inclusion, exaggeration of distance, energetic proximity
- normal: natural spatial relation
- long/tele: compression, isolation, observation
- macro: tactile detail
- deep focus intent: simultaneous relationships
- shallow focus intent: attention isolation

## 7. Camera movement

Movement should have a reason.

- pan/tilt: discover, follow, compare
- dolly/push: intensify, enter thought, reveal
- pull: release, contextualize, isolate
- truck/slide: reveal spatial relation
- crane/rise: expand system/world/aftermath
- orbit: transform relation or emphasize object
- handheld: embodied instability/presence
- locked-off: precision, tension, observation
- follow: preserve action continuity

If camera movement adds no meaning, prefer stillness.

## 8. Screen direction and 180-degree logic

Track left/right movement, gaze and spatial axis.

Crossing the axis is allowed when:
- the camera visibly moves across it,
- a neutral shot resets geography,
- the break is deliberately expressive.

Synthetic generation often drifts left/right orientation; continuity checks must explicitly track it.

## 9. Eyeline

A look implies:
- direction
- target
- distance
- relative height
- timing

Reaction shots and POV shots must preserve that geometry unless the mismatch is intentional.

## 10. Coverage

Coverage is not "generate every possible angle".

Choose enough material to protect:
- performance
- information
- action continuity
- editorial rhythm

Common coverage:
- master
- two-shot/group
- singles
- over-the-shoulders
- inserts
- cutaways
- reactions

For code/motion infographic work, equivalent coverage may be:
- system overview
- component focus
- data close-up
- transformation
- comparison
- return to overview

## 11. Motivated cuts

Cut because something changes.

Useful motivations:
- action
- gaze
- line of dialogue
- sound
- new information
- emotional beat
- graphic match
- spatial reveal
- contrast
- temporal compression

Avoid cuts that exist only because an arbitrary duration expired.

## 12. Edit transitions

- hard cut: direct relation/contrast
- match cut: visual/conceptual continuity
- cut on action: kinetic continuity
- J-cut: incoming sound precedes picture
- L-cut: outgoing sound continues after picture change
- dissolve: temporal/emotional overlap
- wipe/graphic transition: stylized or explanatory relation
- dip/fade: structural punctuation

Transitions are grammar, not decoration.

## 13. Composition

Control:
- subject hierarchy
- negative space
- depth layers
- leading lines
- symmetry/asymmetry
- balance
- headroom/look room
- graphic weight
- readable text-safe area

The most important visual element should win attention without needing explanation.

## 14. Lighting intent

Represent:
- key direction
- softness/hardness
- contrast
- motivation/source
- separation from background
- time/weather
- emotional temperature

For generated footage, lighting continuity must be written into shot contracts; otherwise adjacent shots often disagree.

## 15. Production design

Maintain recurring:
- architecture
- props
- costume/wardrobe
- surface/material vocabulary
- palette
- typography
- symbols
- cultural/period rules

Design carries story information before dialogue does.

## 16. Performance direction

When humans/characters are involved, describe playable intention, not vague emotion.

Weak:
"be sad"

Better:
"hide disappointment while trying to keep the conversation normal"

Track:
- objective
- tactic
- obstacle
- subtext
- physical behavior
- emotional turn

## 17. Sound as direction

Plan sound before finishing picture.

Use:
- dialogue/voice
- room tone
- ambience
- foreground effects
- off-screen cues
- music
- silence

Sound can:
- reveal before picture,
- redirect attention,
- connect locations,
- carry a cut,
- create scale,
- create subjectivity.

## 18. Montage

Use montage when compression itself has meaning.

Specify montage logic:
- chronological
- escalating
- parallel
- associative
- procedural
- contrast
- accumulation

Do not use montage to hide an unclear story.

## 19. Expository / infographic directing

For information-led films, every visual should perform at least one function:

- orient
- identify
- quantify
- compare
- explain mechanism
- show sequence
- show causality
- show hierarchy
- show geography
- show change over time
- show uncertainty

Decorative movement alone does not count.

## 20. Long-form visual grammar

Define patterns that recur across a long work:
- default shot scale
- when camera moves
- when graphics appear
- recurring transition motif
- chapter-open behavior
- interview/documentary treatment
- archival treatment
- map/data treatment
- memory/dream treatment
- climax exceptions

Variation should happen against an established baseline.

## 21. Director's preflight

Before production, confirm:

- story/scene objective exists
- viewer question exists
- POV is intentional
- geography is understandable
- screen direction is tracked
- visual motif is defined
- sound strategy is defined
- transition into next unit is planned
- expensive shots are justified
- no shot duplicates another shot's function without reason
